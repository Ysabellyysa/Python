INTRODUCTION
Reading has always been shaped by the way readers talk about books. Book clubs, classroom discussions, library
recommendations, and online reviews all help readers decide what to read next, understand difficult passages, and
connect a story to their own lives. In recent years, digital reading platforms have grown into large ecosystems
where millions of readers browse catalogs, highlight passages, and write short comments. Despite this growth,
most platforms still rely on simple tools such as star ratings, genre tags, and fixed lists of similar titles. These
tools say little about why a reader enjoyed a book, how a reader feels about a character, or what kind of story a
reader might want after finishing a long novel. As a result, recommendations often feel generic and discussions
remain shallow.
Recent advances in large language models (LLMs) have opened new possibilities for conversational systems that
understand text and respond with fluent, context-aware language. Such models can summarize chapters, explain
themes, compare authors, and imitate the tone of different literary traditions. However, their use as reading
companions is still limited. Existing tools often produce vague summaries, mix up plots and characters, or give
the same advice to every reader. Few studies examine how a language model can be adapted to the specific
vocabulary and style of literary discussion, and even fewer measure whether readers actually find the result useful.
This study investigates the use of a fine-tuned language model as an adaptive reading companion. The system was
trained on a corpus of public domain books, reading guides, and discussion notes, with the goal of producing
answers that are faithful to the text, sensitive to the reader, and pleasant to read. Unlike template-based
recommendation engines, the proposed approach generates original explanations for each question, adjusting its
tone and level of detail to the reader. By testing the system on different reading scenarios and literary genres, the
study explores how language models can support reading without replacing it.
The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. The next section reviews the literature on book
recommendation, computational analysis of literature, and conversational reading tools. The Method section
describes the corpus, the preprocessing steps, the training configuration, and the evaluation design. The Results
section reports the performance of the model across several quality dimensions, including faithfulness to the text,
helpfulness, and response time. The Conclusion summarizes the main contributions and limitations, and outlines
directions for future work on language models and reading.
1. RELATED WORKS
Research on book recommendation began with systems that compared the ratings of different readers. These
collaborative methods work well when many readers have rated the same titles, but they struggle with new books,
rare genres, and readers who have rated very few titles. Content-based methods tried to solve this problem by
describing each book with keywords, subjects, and short summaries. While useful, these descriptions are usually
written by publishers or librarians and rarely capture the experience of reading, such as pacing, mood, or
emotional intensity. Hybrid systems combine both ideas, yet they still treat a book as a small set of labels instead
of a rich piece of language.
Computational literary studies have taken a different path by analyzing the text itself. Researchers have measured
sentence length, vocabulary richness, and the frequency of common words to compare authors and detect changes
in style over time. Network methods have mapped the relations between characters in novels, showing how
protagonists, allies, and rivals are connected across chapters. Topic models have been used to study how themes
rise and fall in large collections of nineteenth century fiction. These studies show that books carry measurable
patterns, but most of them aim at scholars and not at everyday readers who want a simple conversation about a
story.
Language models have recently been applied to creative and educational tasks related to books. Some systems
summarize long novels by splitting them into chapters and combining short summaries into a final overview.
Others generate study questions for students, write alternative endings, or answer questions about a given
passage. Studies of these systems report that fluent output is easy to obtain, but faithfulness is harder to guarantee.
Models may invent events that never happened, attribute quotes to the wrong character, or describe a book they
have never seen with great confidence. Such errors are especially harmful in reading contexts, where a reader
may trust the answer instead of checking the text.
Conversational reading companions form a smaller body of work. Early chatbots for libraries answered simple
questions about opening hours and available titles. More recent prototypes allow readers to discuss a book while
reading it, receive explanations of unfamiliar words, or ask for the meaning of a symbol. Surveys of readers
suggest that people value these tools most when they do not reveal too much of the plot, respect the reader's own
interpretation, and admit uncertainty. Still, few studies fine-tune a model directly on literary material or measure
the effect of such training on style, faithfulness, and speed. This study addresses that gap by building and
evaluating a reading companion trained on a focused collection of books and reading guides.
2. METHODOLOGY
This study follows a case study design to examine how a fine-tuned language model behaves as a reading
companion. The experimental setting was a small reading platform in which readers could ask questions about
books, request recommendations, and discuss themes. A compact open-source language model was chosen as the
base architecture because it offers a good balance between quality, flexibility, and the low computational cost
needed for repeated training runs. The objective was to compare the behavior of the model before and after
fine-tuning on literary material.
The training corpus was compiled from public domain novels, short stories, essays, and reading guides written for
students and book clubs. In total, the dataset contained about 2.4 million tokens drawn from 600 books, 4,000
chapter summaries, 9,000 discussion questions, and 1,200 reader reviews. Only texts that were free to use were
included, and all personal information was removed from the reviews. The collection covered several genres,
including adventure, mystery, romance, science fiction, historical fiction, and poetry, so that the model would meet
a wide range of narrative styles and vocabularies.
Before training, the data passed through a multi-step preprocessing pipeline. Duplicate passages were removed,
page numbers and headers were deleted, and punctuation was normalized. Long books were divided into passages
of similar length that kept paragraphs and dialogue together, so that each training example held a complete idea.
Reading guides and reviews were paired with the passages they discussed, which allowed the model to learn the
link between a piece of text and the way readers talk about it. A small part of the data was set aside for validation
and for the final tests.
The base model was fine-tuned with a learning rate of 3e-5 and a batch size of 32 for 20 training epochs. Dropout
was used to reduce overfitting, and a cosine schedule slowly lowered the learning rate. Checkpoints were saved
after every epoch and compared on the validation set. The final checkpoint was chosen by balancing validation
loss with the quality of sample answers read by the research team. This choice was important because the lowest
validation loss did not always produce the most natural answers.
To evaluate the system, a set of 60 reading scenarios was built. The scenarios covered six types of requests:
recommending a book, summarizing a chapter, explaining a theme, describing a character, comparing two authors,
and explaining a difficult passage. Each scenario was tested on several genres. Faithfulness to the text was judged
by three experienced librarians, who checked each answer against the original book. Helpfulness and readability
were rated by 40 volunteer readers on a 5-point Likert scale. Finally, the time needed to produce each answer was
recorded in order to test whether the system could be used in an interactive setting.
3. RESULTS
The training process was followed over 20 epochs. As shown in Figure 1, both the training loss and the
validation loss fell quickly during the first epochs and then slowed down. The validation loss stayed close to the
training loss until the twelfth epoch, after which a small gap appeared. This gap suggests that the model began to
memorize some passages, which is why the checkpoint from the fifteenth epoch was selected instead of the last
one. Even so, the curves show that the model learned the structure and vocabulary of the corpus in a stable way.
Figure 1: Training and validation loss during fine-tuning.
Faithfulness to the text was the most important quality dimension. According to the librarians, 89.4% of the
answers were consistent with the original books, including the names of characters, the order of events, and the
meaning of key scenes. The remaining answers contained small errors, such as mixing up two minor characters or
placing an event in the wrong chapter. Serious inventions, in which the model described events that never
happened, appeared in only 3% of the answers. Faithfulness was highest for adventure and mystery novels and
lowest for poetry, where meaning depends strongly on interpretation.
Figure 2: Faithfulness scores by literary genre.
Helpfulness was measured by the group of 40 readers. The average rating was 4.4 out of 5, and most answers
received a 4 or a 5. Readers praised the clear explanations of themes and the way the model adjusted its language
for beginners and advanced readers. Recommendations were rated slightly lower than explanations, because some
readers wanted more surprising suggestions and fewer well-known classics. Comments also showed that readers
liked answers that avoided spoilers when asked, which the model did correctly in most cases.
Figure 3: Reader ratings of helpfulness on a 5-point Likert scale.
Style was evaluated by asking the readers whether an answer sounded natural and appropriate for the book being
discussed. The fine-tuned model scored clearly higher than the base model, especially on questions about older
books, where the base model often used a modern and flat tone. When describing a gothic novel, for example, the
fine-tuned model used slower sentences and darker words, while for an adventure story it chose short and lively
sentences. This ability to follow the mood of a book was one of the clearest benefits of fine-tuning on literary
material.
Figure 4: Naturalness and style scores for the base and the fine-tuned model.
Response time was also measured. The average time to produce an answer was 620 milliseconds, with a small
variation between request types. Summaries of long chapters took the longest, while short questions about a
single word or character took the least. All answers were produced in less than two seconds, which is acceptable
for a conversation in which readers type a question and wait for a reply.
Figure 5: Response time by request type.
Error analysis showed that 81% of the answers had no visible problems. Among the remaining answers, 12%
contained small factual mistakes about characters or events, 5% were too long or repeated the same idea, and 2%
gave advice that did not match the reader's request. Although these numbers are low, they show that readers may
still trust a confident answer that is wrong. For this reason, the study recommends that reading companions show
the passage they used, invite readers to check the book, and say clearly when they are not sure.

4. CONCLUSION
This study examined the use of a fine-tuned language model as a reading companion for a digital reading
platform. By training on about 2.4 million tokens of public domain books, reading guides, and discussion notes,
the model reached strong results on several measures. Faithfulness to the text was 89.4%, the average helpfulness
rating given by readers was 4.4 out of 5, and the average response time was 620 milliseconds. These results
suggest that a small and focused model can support reading in a useful, natural, and fast way.
Several limitations remain. The model sometimes confused minor characters, struggled with poetry and highly
symbolic passages, and gave recommendations that were too close to well-known titles. The corpus contained only
public domain material, which limits the model's knowledge of recent books and contemporary writing. The
evaluation also used a limited number of readers and a short period of testing, so the long term effect on reading
habits is still unknown.
Future work should combine the language model with a retrieval system that finds the exact passage needed to
answer a question and shows it to the reader. Reinforcement learning from reader feedback could help the system
learn which explanations are most helpful and which recommendations feel too predictable. It would also be
valuable to test the approach in schools and libraries, to include books in several languages, and to study how
conversations with a reading companion change the time that readers spend with the books themselves. Overall,
this study offers a first step toward reading tools that help readers think and talk about books without taking the
place of the books.
